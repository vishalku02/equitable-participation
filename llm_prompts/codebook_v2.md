### A.1 Unit and coding fields

Unit: A student turn may span several transcript lines, including thinking aloud interrupted by another speaker. Group lines when they develop one mathematical thought. Record Whose on every line that belongs to the turn. Record Level only on the last line of the turn, once the thought is complete.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Whose idea? | Self: the student develops their own mathematical answer, approach, or reasoning. Other: the student builds on a specific peer's mathematical idea. |
| Builds on | For other, record the peer and the line or turn containing the idea being built on. |
| Level | For self: fully detailed, some detail, wrong detail, or no detail. For other: high, medium, or low. |
| Unclear / not coded | Use when the mathematical contribution or its referent cannot be determined, or when the utterance falls outside the construct. Record why. |

Boundary: A reply or progress toward an answer is not automatically co-construction. Ask whether the student builds on a specific peer idea, rather than merely speaking after it. This becomes important during problem-solving.

### A.2 Levels for a student's own idea (self)

| Level | Definition | Example and coding rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Fully detailed | The turn makes the student's approach or solution unambiguous in the context of the activity. During problem-solving, treating a clear, complete proposed approach can be considered "full" even though it may not yet explain why the approach works. | Example from Webb et al.: "So first I did 6 plus 6 19 times. Then I did 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6. And it all equaled 12. But something right here. Is that I had a leftover 6. So then I did 12 plus 12 and that equaled 24. Then I added another 12. Then I figured out that I should add the 12 to the 6 and I got 18. Then 24 + 24 equals 48. Then I did 48 + 48 and that equals 96. Then I did 96 plus 18 and that got me 114." Example from our transcripts (Activity 1: "twice as many cubes"): Student 1 (a6): "Tres piezas de cubos más, no, multipilcados por dos. Es que era igual a seis." Working translation: "Three pieces of cubes plus, no, multiplied by two. It's that it was equal to six." The multiplication by two and result of six make the proposed approach clear. |
| Some detail | The student gives a meaningful part of an approach or explanation, but it is incomplete. | Example from Webb et al.: "What I did, I made a T-chart. And I put the number of students and the number of cranes. So 1 student would equal 6 cranes, 2 students would equal 12 cranes, 3 students would equal 18 cranes, 4 students would equal 24 cranes, 5 students would equal 30 cranes, and so on." Example from our transcripts (Activity 2: Andre has five cubes; Han has four times as many): Student 1 (b26): "I was thinking of put five plus five four times more." This suggests repeated addition using the task's numbers. |
| Wrong detail | The explanation contains an identifiable error. | |
| No detail | The student names an answer or approach but gives no explanation of it. | Example from Webb et al.: "19 groups of 6". Example from our transcripts: Teacher (b68): "So how many did you have over here?" Student 1 (b69): "Twenty." As an isolated answer, it gives no reasoning. |

### A.3 Levels for building on a peer's idea (other)

| Level | Definition | Example and coding rationale |
|---|---|---|
| High | Adds to a specific peer idea—for example, extends it, gives an alternative that explicitly relates to it, or corrects it and develops the correction. | Examples from Webb et al. (names anonymized): Student 1 offers Student 2 an "easier" way to add 16/10 + 17/10. Student 1 corrects Student 2's idea of a whole. Student 1 and Student 2 catch their error together. Example from our transcripts: Following Student 1's multiplication explanation (a6), Student 2 offers an additive approach. Student 2 (a20–a21): "Yo lo hice igualito, pero con dibujo diferente. Esta vez no usé la multiplicación, solo plus. Yo hice plus con tres y plus con tres. Y eso hizo seis." Working translation: "I did it exactly the same, but with a different drawing. This time I didn't use multiplication, just plus. I did plus with three and plus with three. And that made six." Student 2 explicitly relates the additive approach to Student 1's multiplication approach. |
| Medium | Refers to a specific detail of a peer's idea but does not develop an alternative or explanation. | Examples from Webb et al.: Student 1 explains how Student 2 divided the sandwich into sixths. Student 1 describes Student 2's drawing of fourths. Example from our transcripts: Student 2 (b47): "Cuatro, cuatro por cuatro" ("Four, four times four"). Student 1 (b49): "Cinco por cuatro manín" ("Five times four, bro"). Student 1 supplies a specific correction without a reason. |
| Low | Responds to a peer's idea only generally, without engaging its mathematical details. | Examples from Webb et al. (names anonymized): Student 1: "I am still not convinced about Student 2's idea." Student 1: "Student 2's looks more similar." Example from our transcripts: After Student 1's cube-and-multiplication explanation (a6), Student 2 (a7) says, "Ya veo" ("I see"). |

### A.4 Decision rules for difficult turns

- Questions. Do not code a starter or generic prompt on its own unless it contains a mathematical idea. A follow-up question can be engagement when it refers to a particular idea or presses on its reasoning. Judge the question in its surrounding turn.
- Thinking aloud while solving. Keep an unfinished question with the student's developing turn when appropriate.
- Answers to questions. A bare answer such as "twenty" can be self–no detail when it states the student's answer. Teacher-led known-answer exchanges are treated as self rather than peer co-construction.
- Keep fragments and pointing in context. Read a stretches of thinking with the surrounding approach when there is evidence of what it represents. Otherwise mark it unclear.
- Facilitating the work. For now, do not separately code participation or coordination moves.
